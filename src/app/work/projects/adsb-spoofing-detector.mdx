## What it is

Every aircraft constantly broadcasts its identity, position, speed and altitude over an open radio protocol called ADS-B, on 1090 MHz. Those broadcasts are unauthenticated and unencrypted: anyone with cheap hardware can forge them and inject "ghost" aircraft that were never there. It's a well-known, still-unsolved weakness in aviation.

I wanted to see how far I could get against it on a student budget, so I bought a 27-euro RTL-SDR dongle, captured real air traffic over the Rhine-Main area, and built a machine-learning layer that flags when the data defies physics. Everything here runs end-to-end on data I captured myself. It's receive-only; every spoofing attack is simulated in software and never transmitted.

## How it works

The whole pipeline runs from a single receiver:

1. **Capture:** the RTL-SDR dongle listens on 1090 MHz; `dump1090` decodes the raw bursts into ADS-B messages.
2. **Build features:** messages are grouped into per-aircraft tracks, and each movement step is reduced to three physics-grounded numbers: ground speed (km/h), climb rate (ft/s) and step distance (km).
3. **Train:** an Isolation Forest learns the shape of *normal* flight from the real data, unsupervised, so it needs no labelled attacks.
4. **Attack:** synthetic spoofs (teleports, impossible speeds, altitude jumps) are generated in software and thrown at the detector.
5. **Evaluate and harden:** performance is measured on held-out data, weaknesses are found by stress-testing, and the feature set is hardened.

## Results

On a held-out test set the model never saw during training:

- **100% detection** of the synthetic attacks (310 of 310).
- **1.6% false-alarm rate** on real flights (5 of 310).
- **98.4% precision.**

## The red-team catch

The first version wrongly flagged a normal *climbing* aircraft. The cause was the altitude feature: it used the raw altitude change and ignored the time gap. Climbing 1,500 ft over 30 seconds is routine; over 3 seconds it's impossible, but the feature couldn't tell them apart. Switching to climb *rate* (ft/s) removed the false alarm while still catching every attack. Finding and fixing that is the whole point of red-teaming your own system.

## Where it breaks

I kept making the attacks subtler until the detector started to miss them, because knowing *why* a system breaks matters more than a clean 100%:

- **Speed:** fakes below about 1,100 km/h, inside the range of real aircraft I observed, slip through; detection only becomes reliable above ~1,800 km/h.
- **Altitude:** the detector is over-sensitive and flags even mild climbs, so it would false-alarm on real climbing and descending aircraft.

Both trace back to the training data: my captures were dominated by high-altitude cruise traffic, so the model knows cruise speed well but rarely saw altitude changes. It's a data-coverage problem, not a model flaw.

## What I learned

- **Movement, not position, is what gives a spoof away.** Speed, climb rate and step distance encode physics a forger has to respect.
- **Time context matters.** The same altitude change is normal or impossible depending on how long it took; the feature has to carry that.
- **Breaking your own system is the real work.** A 100% headline number hid two genuine blind spots that only subtle, graduated attacks exposed.
- **Your model is only as broad as your data.** Cruise-heavy captures produced a cruise-heavy detector.
